### [SEAR: Self-Evolving Agentic Robotics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18792v1)

- **Authors:** Xinyi Wang, Bangzheng Li, Hanyu Wang, Lin Zhang, Andrea Yaoyun Cui, Zhenggang Tang, Shyamal Buch, Dejia Xu
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.RO, cs.AI, cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18792v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18792v1)

Frontier language-model agents can now improve robots on their own: they write control code, test it, and keep what works as skills and memories for the next task. We introduce SEAR (Self-Evolving Agentic Robotics), a framework and benchmark built around two questions. Is the agent really learning, or just trying more often? And does what it learns still help on new tasks, in a new simulator, and on a real robot? The framework runs an agent in three modes: it writes code that becomes the policy (code-as-policy), acts live in the control loop (agent-as-policy), or works as a researcher that fine-tunes a vision-language-action model (auto-research). The benchmark adds controls that separate retained experience from repeated attempts, familiar tasks, privileged simulator state, larger budgets, and luck. Across seven frontier language models, 330 simulated and five physical tasks, and over four years of agent runtime, we highlight three findings. First, the learning curve of a robotic agent depends both on the overall time budget and the task difficulty relative to model capability. Harder tasks make longer sessions worthwhile; on easier tasks, re-rolling short attempts does just as well. Second, control code and shared routines transfer across tasks and simulators, while task-specific solutions do not. On a real robot, we observe a performance gap between agent-designed practice and novel human-designed scenes. Third, different models evolve more effectively in different ways. Some do best acting live, others writing code first. And a text-only model keeps pace with a comparable multimodal one: perception helps with error assessment, but agentic reasoning and tool use can matter more. We release the benchmark and codebase to support future research.

### [MFG: Multi-turn Feedback Guided Molecular Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18851v1)

- **Authors:** Trieu Nguyen, Frazier N. Baker, Ruoxi Gao, Daniel Adu-Ampratwum, Xia Ning
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI, cs.LG, q-bio.QM
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18851v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18851v1)

Multi-objective molecular optimization is a critical process in drug discovery that aims to modify a given molecule to simultaneously improve multiple properties while maintaining a similar overall structure. This process often requires iterative modifications guided by computational or experimental feedback. Recent large language model (LLM)-based methods have begun to explore this iterative approach; however, these methods are not explicitly trained to leverage such feedback, while existing approaches primarily focus on single-turn molecular optimization. To address this gap, we introduce Multi-turn Feedback Guided (MFG) molecular optimization, a reinforcement learning framework that trains an LLM to progressively optimize molecules using feedback from previous turns. MFG introduces a feedback vector capturing both property improvements and source molecule similarity, alongside a feedback-aligned reward function that promotes progressive improvement across turns. We apply MFG over $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O}$, a state-of-the-art LLM model for multi-objective molecular optimization, evaluate $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O-MFG}$ on ten multi-objective molecular optimization tasks. The results demonstrate that $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O-MFG}$ achieves the highest average similarity-constrained success rate ($81.50%$), outperforming the base model $\text{GeLLM}^{3}\text{O}$ ($70.81%$).

### [Can an Agent Learn to Predict Its Own Failure? Outcome-Grounded Confidence Calibration in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18614v1)

- **Authors:** Ashutosh Hathidara
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI, cs.LG
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18614v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18614v1)

Controlling an acting LLM agent means deciding, at each step of a long-horizon task, whether to trust its next action, abstain, or hand off to a human. That decision needs a per-step confidence signal predicting whether the current rollout will eventually succeed. The agent's own self-reported verbalized confidence is the natural candidate, but existing reinforcement learning methods calibrate it against the correctness of a decision at the current step. We propose a method to calibrate it against the eventual outcome of a multi-step episode: under GRPO, the group of rollouts for a task supplies success labels, and a proper scoring rule scores each step's confidence against the realized outcome. In offline RL on recorded trajectories of a Qwen2.5-7B agent in ALFWorld, TextWorld, and WebShop environments, the AUROC of step confidence against episode outcome rises from near chance to 0.68-0.85, and the top-10% of steps the agent is most confident about succeed ~4.3x more often than average. In online RL on the live ALFWorld agent, four calibration rewards improve early-step AUROC, expected calibration error goes from 0.42 to below 0.12, and selective execution improves over both the untrained agent and a format-only RL control, without degrading task success. We also introduce an evaluation protocol that separates episode depth, task difficulty, and attempt-level foresight, which shows where the gain lies: the trained agent recognizes from its first step which tasks it will fail, while telling a succeeding attempt from a failing one at the same task is harder, with some early-window signal emerging on TextWorld.

### [How Low Can You Go: Fine-Tuning LLMs with Bayesian Optimization (LlMBO)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18504v1)

- **Authors:** Aditya Kulkarni, Davide Sartor, Carlos Soto, Nathan Wycoff
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** stat.ML, cs.LG, stat.ME
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18504v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18504v1)

Recently, Morris et al. introduced TinyLoRA, a strategy for LLM fine-tuning which introduces arbitrarily few parameters, and demonstrated accuracy improvements using less than $10$ parameters rather than the ${\gtrsim 10,000}$ typically demanded by LoRA-based methods. However, that study continued to use gradient-based local search methods of the kind deployed for training multi-billion parameter LLMs in the first place. This article is predicated on the observation that, in the low dimensional setting, global optimization methods become tractable, unlocking Large language Model Bayesian Optimization (LlMBO). LlMBO is sample-efficient, gradient-free, memory-light, parallelizes across devices without synchronization, and, unlike policy-gradient methods, can optimize greedy-decoding accuracy directly. We describe numerical experiments comparing LlMBO against GRPO on fine-tuning tasks for a variety of models on a math benchmark suite which show that LlMBO can be a suitable alternative in this regime, matching or exceeding GRPO's fine tuning gains, while using a fraction of the compute and memory.

### [Copying Is Not Grounding: Training Faithful Long-Form RAG with Authored-Sentence Credit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18442v1)

- **Authors:** Hoang Pham, Duc Anh Vu, Duy Tung Doan, Cong-Duy T Nguyen, Anh Tuan Luu
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18442v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18442v1)

In search engines and assistants, long-form RAG answers can mislead readers with claims their sources do not support. Faithfulness rewards, the share of supported sentences, train models toward fewer such claims. We show that these rewards are hacked by copying. A sentence copied from the passages is supported almost by construction, so trained models learn to copy rather than ground what they write. Common defenses only move the hack: a KL or copy penalty leaves copying in place or trades it for shorter, emptier answers and refusals. GRPO, the standard RL algorithm, makes it worse by giving every sentence its answer's advantage, reinforcing copied and grounded sentences alike. We propose Authored-Sentence Credit (ASC) , which fixes both by moving GRPO's reward and advantage from the answer to the sentence level. The reward credits only sentences that are authored rather than copied, say more than the question, and are supported by the passages. The advantage compares each sentence with every sentence sampled for the question, so one that earns no credit ranks last and is never reinforced. Across four models and two in-domain, three held-out corpora, only ASC lowers the error rate of authored sentences and writes more supported ones in every setting, by 38% and 46% at the median under held-out evaluators, giving readers more grounded information. It takes none of these shortcuts, and two LLM judges comparing whole answers prefer no baseline to the base model but rate ASC's answers on par with it. We conclude that a faithfulness reward should credit what a model writes, not what it copies.

### [Do Factuality Methods Make LLMs More Factual? Reproducibility and Fair Comparison](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18415v1)

- **Authors:** Pratuat Amatya, Vinay Setty, V Venktesh, Avishek Anand
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CL, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18415v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18415v1)

Large Language Models (LLMs) are aligned to human preferences, after pre-training and supervised fine-tuning steps. However, standard RL alignment steps such as RLHF do not focus on factuality of LLMs, hence they may generate non-factual information, which could result in unintended consequences. In response, a number of factuality alignment approaches have been proposed in the literature which claim to improve factuality of LLMs on their own benchmarks. However, these methods have not been reproduced independently and are also not comparable since each uses a different base model, training data, evaluation set, judge, and metric. In this paper, we present the first independent reproduction of five factuality alignment methods, which span on-policy RL (PPO, GRPO, and Dr.~GRPO) and offline preference optimization (DPO and KTO), and compare them on a common benchmark with common evaluation metrics. We found that none of these methods reproduces in full, and on the common benchmark none of the methods significantly outperforms the base model. On closer inspection, the rewards optimize for format, length, or brevity rather than factuality and relevance, whereas the choice between on-policy and offline training matters little. Furthermore, the evaluation metrics and benchmarks are also part of the problem since they do not measure relevance of text to the prompt. Since there is no ground truth associated with the prompts, the reliance on LLM judges and retrieval for verification also makes the evaluation fragile. We release our code, data, and checkpoints.

### [Prompt or Policy? Optimizing Physician Slate Presentations with LLM User Simulators](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18309v1)

- **Authors:** Andrei Niculae, Adrian Cosma, Emilian Radoi
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18309v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18309v1)

Choosing the right physician is a key challenge for patients seeking medical care. Physician recommendation systems identify relevant doctors, but patients still find it difficult to compare their profiles to make a decision. We formulate slate presentation optimization: given a patient's medical case and a fixed, ranked list of physicians, we generate short, case-specific summaries that make doctors easier to compare, while remaining grounded in the information in their profiles. To optimize physician presentations without relying on real patient interactions, we develop an offline framework that uses persona-conditioned LLMs to simulate patient choices. We generate patient personas from attributes extracted from each medical case and evaluate presentations for factual accuracy, relevance, naturalness and simulated patient choice. We also use a second LLM judge, unseen during optimization, to assess simulator-specific overfitting. We compare automatic prompt-level optimization with GEPA, parameter-level optimization with GRPO, and their combination across two model sizes. GRPO substantially outperforms GEPA in satisfying the full set of constraints, and its gains in simulated conversion persist under the unseen judge. GEPA alone brings smaller improvements, but using its optimized prompt to initialize GRPO speeds up training and achieves the highest simulated conversion. These results show that prompt-level and parameter-level optimization play complementary roles in improving how physician recommendations are presented.

### [Teach Me After You Learn It: Lookahead Self-Distillation for RLVR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18256v1)

- **Authors:** Zepeng Zhai, Meilin Chen
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18256v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18256v1)

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) has emerged as a popular training paradigm for LLMs, providing dense, token-level supervision by aligning a model's own distribution with the distribution induced by privileged information. However, existing OPSD methods derive supervision solely from privileged information of the same instance, leaving inter-instance supervision largely unexplored. We propose Lookahead Self-Distillation (LEAD), which introduces inter-instance distillation by allowing the teacher to first learn from other on-policy rollouts before teaching the student. LEAD constructs lookahead teachers through virtual RL updates on disjoint subsets of on-policy rollouts, and uses each teacher to provide cross-group token-level supervision for the other subset. To reconcile the complementary yet potentially conflicting signals from OPSD and RLVR, we introduce harmonious modulation, which adaptively modulates the GRPO advantage based on the agreement between the two signals. Extensive experiments on scientific reasoning and interactive-agent benchmarks show that LEAD achieves the best in-domain average performance across model scales while maintaining out-of-domain generalization comparable to GRPO.

### [Many Right Answers, One Training Example: Removing Responder Noise from GRPO for LLM Recommenders](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17771v1)

- **Authors:** Xin Li, Hao Jiang, Jinghao Guo, Weisi Lin, Chau Yuen
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17771v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17771v1)

A recommendation request on Reddit draws several replies, each naming different titles. When an LLM recommender is trained on such threads with reinforcement learning (RL), which replies should a generated list be rewarded against? The state-of-the-art recipe, Rank-GRPO, picks one reply per training example, so the advantage mostly records which reply was drawn (85% of its variance) rather than how good the list is. We argue that RL should train on the request, not on each answer, and propose ReMaRk: it scores every list once against the union of all replies' titles and samples each request equally. This makes RL cheaper. On seven backbones and three datasets, ReMaRk reaches the validation recall of 2,000 per-reply RL steps in 25 to 53% of the steps and leads at that budget; trained until neither improves, it is never significantly behind Rank-GRPO and in four of seven pairs reaches Rank-GRPO's best with fewer GPU-hours. The same reward helps on ProtoQA, where about 100 people answer each question, which points to a simple rule for RL with several valid human answers: reward them jointly.

### [CoCA: Criterion-Aware Credit Assignment for Rubric-Based Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17733v1)

- **Authors:** Guangcheng Zhu, Yingfan Ma, Xing Zheng, Haobo Wang, Shenzhi Yang, Kai Tang, Zhengqing Zang, Bowen Song, Weiqiang Wang, Gang Chen
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17733v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17733v1)

Rubric-based reinforcement learning (RL) evaluates responses against explicit criteria to construct rewards, offering a viable way to train large language models on open-ended tasks. However, standard methods compress criterion-level feedback into scalar rewards and assign uniform token advantages, leaving structured rubric supervision underutilized. Recent work seeks finer-grained guidance by treating rubrics as privileged information to derive self-distillation targets, but the resulting learning signal is not grounded in criterion outcomes and can lead to reward degradation. This motivates us to reframe the problem as criterion-aware credit assignment, where privileged rubric information serves as a relevance cue for credit allocation rather than a source of imitation targets. Accordingly, we propose CoCA, which derives criterion-level credits from group-relative outcomes and allocates them across tokens based on criterion-induced distribution shifts, yielding both outcome-grounded and fine-grained learning signals. Empirically, CoCA achieves an average gain of +4.2% over standard rubric-based RL across medicine and science benchmarks, demonstrating more effective learning on open-ended tasks.

### [Calibrating LLM Explanations to Human Decisions with Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17306v2)

- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17306v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17306v2)

Explanations of LLM outputs are considered an important way to make model behavior legible, helping human users make good decisions on downstream tasks. However, default training approaches for improving explanations target model task performance or human preference rather than human decision quality. To bridge this gap, we introduce \textbf{HD-RL}, a reinforcement learning framework for optimizing LLM explanations for human decision making. Our approach calibrates a human decision simulator on observed human decisions, applying a proxy reward model to optimize the LLM to generate explanations that help people to make good decisions. ### [ATTEST: Evidence-Grounded Reinforcement Learning for Long-Audio Temporal Understanding](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17149v1)

- **Authors:** Ashutosh Chaubey, Jinxi Guo, Minh Tran, Michael Andersland, Ozlem Kalinli
- **Published:** 2026-10-12
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.SD
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17149v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17149v1)

Temporal grounding is fundamental to audio understanding, yet large audio language models (LALMs) remain weak at it on long audio, where hallucinations compound and annotated data is scarce. Existing benchmarks cover only short clips or offer coarse, synthetic annotations. We introduce EgoSCAPE, a manually verified benchmark created from Ego4D with 700 real audios of durations up to 5 minutes, annotated with dense captions, sound sources, audio timelines, and temporal grounding queries. We further propose ATTEST, a GRPO-based reinforcement learning framework that improves temporal grounding without changing the base model's architecture or token vocabulary. ATTEST combines (i) a counterfactual grounding reward penalizing predictions that survive removal of their acoustic evidence, (ii) an attention-density reward encouraging generation to attend to its own claimed time spans, and (iii) token-wise advantages giving segment-level credit for long dense captions. ATTEST outperforms prior temporal grounding methods on existing dense captioning and grounding benchmarks, with fewer hallucinations. Together, EgoSCAPE and ATTEST establish a rigorous evaluation-training recipe that brings temporally grounded audio understanding closer to practical, long-form deployment.

### [Efficient Resource-Shiftable RL Post-training with Generative Rewards](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16866v1)

- **Authors:** Yanfeng Zhao, Haoyang Li, Sheng Lin, Yuming Zhou, Jiawei Jiang, Bin Cui, Fangcheng Fu
- **Published:** 2026-10-12
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.DC
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16866v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.16866v1)

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training with generative reward models (GenRM) has become a central recipe for improving large language models (LLMs), and it is also increasingly resource-intensive: policy rollout, GenRM-based reward evaluation, and policy training all compete for GPU resources. However, existing colocated and disaggregated systems rely on static resource allocation for these roles. Such allocation cannot adapt as each role's workload changes during RL and therefore leaves a role's resources underutilized. We present PivotRL, an RL post-training system that dynamically shifts resources among different roles according to their runtime demands. PivotRL introduces a resource-shiftable runtime built on a Global Parameter Storage, together with a unified mechanism that efficiently shifts role-specific context. ### [TPPO: Tracking-Value PPO For Language Model Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16794v1)

- **Authors:** Futing Wang, Chenxi Li, Jianhao Yan, Yun Luo, Zhi Wang, Yizhuo Li, Runzhe Zhan, Yu Cheng, Tao Lin
- **Published:** 2026-10-12

- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16794v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.16794v1)

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is widely used in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards for language-model reasoning. While \emph{value evolution along trajectory} characterizes relative token-level credit, yet standard critics often fail to track it under sparse terminal rewards. To model this evolution, we analyze the relation between successive prefix values and show that they admit a Bayesian decomposition with additive updates in log-odds space. We use this additive structure to construct Tracking-Value PPO (\method), a cumulative value parameterization that sums position-wise scalar outputs into value logits. ### [GraphOPD: Graph-Augmented On-Policy Distillation for LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08959v2)

- **Authors:** Bohan Lin, Liyi Chen, Zhuoning Guo, Muyang Li, Qimeng Wang, Yan Gao, Yao Hu, Yudong Zhang
- **Published:** 2026-10-06
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08959v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.08959v2)

On-policy distillation post-trains large language model agents by supplying dense, step-level guidance from a teacher policy when the reinforcement-learning reward is sparse and arrives only once per trajectory. Existing instantiations allocate this guidance by the size of the teacher-student divergence at each step, on the single-turn intuition that a large disagreement marks a mistake worth correcting. Once decisions chain over many turns, that rule misfires, since an early drift enters every later context both policies condition on, leaving the teacher consistent with the drifted trajectory instead of flagging its cause, while interchangeable steps register large but outcome-irrelevant divergences. We demonstrate this on an agentic benchmark, where distilling the highest-divergence steps brings no consistent benefit over random selection. To this end, we introduce GraphOPD, the first method to bring graph-based structural augmentation into on-policy distillation for agent capabilities. It reads which steps enabled which later ones from the environment's own record of state changes, immune to the drift that corrupts the teacher-student gap, organizes them into a dependency graph, scores each step by a random-walk stationary distribution over it, and fuses that structural credit with the divergence signal into a trajectory-relative mask concentrating supervision on each rollout's highest-aptitude steps. Across three model scales and eleven baselines on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SearchQA, GraphOPD shows competitive performance throughout, improving over the strongest baseline by up to +5.8 pp. An executed-replay audit further shows that this structural credit score tracks true causal impact far above chance, that both fused signals are independently necessary, and that the same signal transfers to out-of-domain tool-integrated reasoning.

### [Reinforcement Learning with Metacognitive Feedback Elicits Faithful Uncertainty Expression in LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.32032v2)

- **Authors:** Gabrielle Kaili-May Liu, Avi Caciularu, Gal Yona, Idan Szpektor, Arman Cohan
- **Published:** 2026-06-30
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv.org
- **Affiliations:** Yale University
- **Categories:** cs.CL, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.32032v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.32032v2)

Metacognition is a critical component of intelligence that describes the ability to monitor and regulate one's own cognitive processes. Yet LLMs exhibit systemic deficiencies in key metacognitive faculties: they hallucinate with high confidence, fail to recognize knowledge boundaries, and misrepresent their internal uncertainty--undermining trustworthiness and reliability. Since monitoring task performance and adapting behavior accordingly are central to metacognition, we posit that models capable of accurately judging their own performance are better positioned to improve it. We operationalize this idea via two novel mechanisms: reinforcement learning with metacognitive feedback (RLMF), a paradigm to refine completion rankings during preference optimization based on the quality of a model's self-judgments of performance, and metacognitive data selection, which uses similar self-judgments to identify high-value training examples, outperforming naive active learning. We apply these innovations to the problem of faithful calibration (FC), a task that is itself fundamentally metacognitive: the goal is to align expressed with intrinsic uncertainty, difficult even for frontier LLMs. We adopt a two-stage, decoupled approach, first using these methods to calibrate the faithfulness of models' self-reported confidence scores, then mapping to natural, context-adaptable linguistic uncertainty via targeted output editing. Extensive experiments show RLMF achieves generalizable, state-of-the-art FC on diverse tasks while preserving accuracy. Further, RLMF surpasses standard RL by up to 63% while enhancing models' ability to assess and express their own capability limits. This positions RLMF as a promising paradigm to enhance LLM metacognition toward improved abilities and alignment, and suggests metacognitive performance as an effective RL signal to overcome limits of prior intrinsic feedback methods.

### [Beyond Reward Engineering: A Data Recipe for Long-Context Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18831v2)

- **Authors:** Xiaoyue Xu, Sikui Zhang, Xiaorong Wang, Xu Han, Chaojun Xiao
- **Published:** 2026-06-17
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv.org
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CL, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18831v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.18831v2)

Long-context reasoning is an essential capability for large language models, particularly when they are deployed as autonomous agents that must reason over lengthy trajectories. Reinforcement learning (RL) has recently emerged as a dominant paradigm for improving this ability, yet existing work largely focuses on reward engineering while diverse training data remains scarce. We revisit this problem from a data-centric perspective and show that a simple yet effective data recipe alone, paired with a minimal outcome-based GRPO setup, suffices to substantially improve long-context reasoning. Our recipe targets three complementary task families -- retrieval, multi-evidence synthesis, and reasoning -- for which we construct and curate eight datasets totaling ~14K examples. Experiments on three models (Qwen3-4B/8B/30B-A3B) yield average gains of +7.2/+3.2/+6.4 points across seven long-context benchmarks, surpassing prior RL training sets. We further demonstrate that these gains transfer to agentic tasks, where continuing RL training on an agent-tuned model with our data recipe improves GAIA by +4.8 and BrowseComp by +7.0 points. We will release our datasets to facilitate future research.

### [Improving Heart-Focused Medical Question Answering in LLMs via Variance-Aware Rubric Rewards with GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05174v2)

- **Authors:** Arash Ahmadi, Parisa Masnadi Khiabani, Sarah Sharif, Charles Nicholson, David Ebert, Aswathy K. Vaikom House, Mike Banad
- **Published:** 2026-04-17
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv.org
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CL, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05174v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.05174v2)

Large language models can support medical question answering, but privacy, cost, and reliability still limit their clinical use in focused domains. Can rubric-based reinforcement learning bring a locally deployable model close to much larger systems? We study Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for heart-focused medical question answering with rubric supervision from RaR-Medicine. Rather than rely on binary rubric aggregation or a single overall score, our variance-aware reward framework builds continuous rewards from criterion-level outcomes. It preserves partial credit and reduces reward sparsity. We adapt a Qwen3-14B policy with low-rank adapters and quantized weights. On a held-out heart-related HealthBench subset, the best GRPO variant improves accuracy from 0.362 to 0.502 and F1 from 0.532 to 0.668 over the base model, close to GPT-OSS-120B. An independent clinician rated most responses clinically acceptable (61%) and patient ready (63%), both above the automated accuracy; fewer than 2% were unsafe. Taken together, the results support rubric-based reinforcement learning as a practical route for focused medical language models.

### [Process Rewards for Outcome-Guided Reasoning Steps in LLM Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02341v2)

- **Authors:** Mohammad Rezaei, Jens Lehmann, Sahar Vahdati
- **Published:** 2026-02-08
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv.org
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02341v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.02341v2)

Mathematical reasoning in large language models has improved substantially with reinforcement learning using verifiable rewards, where final answers can be checked automatically and converted into reliable training signals. Most such pipelines optimize outcome correctness only, which yields sparse feedback for long, multi-step solutions and offers limited guidance on intermediate reasoning errors. Recent work therefore introduces process reward models (PRMs) to score intermediate steps and provide denser supervision. In practice, PRM scores are often imperfectly aligned with final correctness and can reward locally fluent reasoning that still ends in an incorrect answer. When optimized as absolute rewards, such signals can amplify fluent failure modes and induce reward hacking. We propose PROGRS, a framework that leverages PRMs while keeping outcome correctness dominant. PROGRS treats process rewards as relative preferences within outcome groups rather than absolute targets. We introduce outcome-conditioned centering, which shifts PRM scores of incorrect trajectories to have zero mean within each prompt group. It removes systematic bias while preserving informative rankings. PROGRS combines a frozen quantile-regression PRM with a multi-scale coherence evaluator. We integrate the resulting centered process bonus into Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) without auxiliary objectives or additional trainable components. Across MATH-500, AMC, AIME, MinervaMath, and OlympiadBench, PROGRS consistently improves Pass@1 over outcome-only baselines and achieves stronger performance with fewer rollouts. These results show that outcome-conditioned centering enables safe and effective use of process rewards for mathematical reasoning.

### [Can an Agent Learn to Predict Its Own Failure? Outcome-Grounded Confidence Calibration in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18614v1)

- **Authors:** Ashutosh Hathidara
- **Published:** 2026-10-13
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI, cs.LG
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18614v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18614v1)

Controlling an acting LLM agent means deciding, at each step of a long-horizon task, whether to trust its next action, abstain, or hand off to a human. That decision needs a per-step confidence signal predicting whether the current rollout will eventually succeed. The agent's own self-reported verbalized confidence is the natural candidate, but existing reinforcement learning methods calibrate it against the correctness of a decision at the current step. We propose a method to calibrate it against the eventual outcome of a multi-step episode: under GRPO, the group of rollouts for a task supplies success labels, and a proper scoring rule scores each step's confidence against the realized outcome. In offline RL on recorded trajectories of a Qwen2.5-7B agent in ALFWorld, TextWorld, and WebShop environments, the AUROC of step confidence against episode outcome rises from near chance to 0.68-0.85, and the top-10% of steps the agent is most confident about succeed ~4.3x more often than average. In online RL on the live ALFWorld agent, four calibration rewards improve early-step AUROC, expected calibration error goes from 0.42 to below 0.12, and selective execution improves over both the untrained agent and a format-only RL control, without degrading task success. We also introduce an evaluation protocol that separates episode depth, task difficulty, and attempt-level foresight, which shows where the gain lies: the trained agent recognizes from its first step which tasks it will fail, while telling a succeeding attempt from a failing one at the same task is harder, with some early-window signal emerging on TextWorld.
