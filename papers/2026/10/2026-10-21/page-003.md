### [The Self-Improvement Problem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21646v1)

- **Authors:** Brian B. Moser, Stanislav Frolov, Tobias C. Nauen, Federico Raue, Andreas Dengel
- **Published:** 2026-10-15
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.LG
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21646v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21646v1)

With the rise of large language models, agentic systems are becoming increasingly autonomous, as they write code, call tools, revise prompts, and propose modifications to their own components. This autonomy raises a fundamental question for safe self-improvement: can an automated gate like a reward model or an automated reviewer reliably decide whether a proposed update should be accepted? The difficulty is structural. The gate must be a terminating, computable procedure, while the property we ultimately care about, namely whether an update is a genuine semantic improvement, depends on potentially unbounded program behavior. We formalize this mismatch by modeling agents as programs and defining improvement in terms of a semantic score that need not be computable. We study two evaluation abstractions. The first is a certifier that approves or rejects an update. The second is a search procedure that, given access to a proposal generator, attempts to output some improving candidate if one exists. We show that, in the fully general setting, no terminating procedure can simultaneously guarantee reliability (never accepting a non-improvement) and non-conservatism (never missing an available improvement): self-referential constructions can force any such gate or searcher to fail. Randomized gates with bounded error face the same limit. We then identify a standard decidable regime used in practice (bounded evaluation and a finite proposal budget) under which exhaustive simulation yields a terminating search procedure with the desired guarantees. We also show the limit of this recovery: a chain of accepted updates can raise the bounded score in every round while the true score falls. Our results draw a boundary between unbounded, universal settings where correctness guarantees collapse and bounded settings where they can be recovered by design.

### [MetaExp: Learning to Self-Improve through Experience via Verifiable Cross-Task Environment Scaling](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21492v1)

- **Authors:** Renhao Li, Xinjie Shen, Yang Su, Jianhong Tu, Derek F. Wong, Dayiheng Liu, Min Yang
- **Published:** 2026-10-15
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21492v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21492v1)

Language-model agents can accumulate interaction feedback, but reliably turning that feedback into reusable experience remains challenging. Existing methods either optimize external experience scaffolds or train experience-related behavior on specific task distributions, leaving open whether experience learning itself can become a transferable model capability. Training such a capability calls for supervision beyond isolated tasks, which mainly support in-episode problem solving rather than revealing what knowledge remains useful across episodes. In this work, we introduce MetaExp, a scalable framework that trains this capability through verifiable task families with controlled cross-task structure. Each family preserves a reusable factor while varying task-specific realizations, enabling transfer without direct solution reuse. We propose two complementary designs: Shared-Tool families keep black-box tools fixed across tasks to elicit operational experience, while Shared-Workflow families preserve latent procedures across changing realizations to elicit procedural experience. We scale environments under both designs, collect and filter sequential interaction trajectories, and fine-tune Qwen3.5-122B-A10B on 4,956 trajectories from 1,000 task families. With parameters frozen during downstream evolution, MetaExp improves by +8.5 points on SpreadsheetBench, peaks at +2.3 on APEX-Agents, and improves by +14.8 on AutomationBench. The gains persist under domain-excluded training, supporting experience learning as a transferable capability for continued non-parametric improvement after deployment.

### [What Survives Self-Evolution: Provenance Marks in Copied Agent Harnesses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21458v1)

- **Authors:** Jieyu Li, Jingya Li, Sheng Li, Xinpeng Zhang, Zhenxing Qian
- **Published:** 2026-10-15
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CR
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21458v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21458v1)

Agent harnesses, the prompt and tool code around a base model, leak. Claude Code's 512,000 lines were mirrored within hours of a packaging error. A plagiarist can pass a leaked harness through a published self-evolver and ship the result as its own. We define this as a covert rewriting attack. No one targets a provenance mark, yet a large language model (LLM) proposer rewrites the file that carries it and a score keeps the better rewrites. We give the first measurement of marks under this attack, with three evolvers, five proposers and two environments. Published marks fail. KGW in the prompt survives GEPA's rewrites in 0 of 12 copies, AgentMark as released in 5 of 12. The proposer's habits decide survival, by two orders of magnitude across models, and selection protects a mark only when each decision sees enough games. We then build a mark for this attack. Its key is the order of near-tied entries of a table of where objects are found, built from public games, so deleting the table costs success, rewrites that keep its rows keep the order, and a customer reads the key from the agent's actions alone. On held-out games it is detected in 62 of 76 copies whose lineage rewrote its file. A scheme-aware plagiarist reshuffles the ties at no measurable cost, and a plagiarist who asks the proposer for more general code removes the key in 75-100% of rewrites.

### [Athanor: An Agent-Native System for Autonomous Post-Training Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21055v1)

- **Authors:** Yufa Zhou, Yichen Wang, Jiakun Fan, Ziqi Shu, Zhichao Liu, Anru R. Zhang, Neiwen Ling, Zijun Wei
- **Published:** 2026-10-15
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.LG, cs.MA
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21055v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.21055v1)

Recursive self-improvement, in which AI systems build stronger AI systems, is one promising route to superintelligence. We study a weaker form that can be measured today: frontier agents post-training a small raw base model with public data, as a test of their research potential. We build Athanor, an open agent-native system for autonomous post-training research (AutoPostTrain) on real GPUs. We give the agents full research autonomy, low-friction engineering tools, and efficient starter code. The agents carry out the research themselves: they read datasets and evaluation results, build their training data, write and modify training algorithms, tune hyperparameters, and analyze the results to decide what to try next. They work in a sandbox and cannot use external teacher models. Athanor's services supply frozen evaluation, managed jobs, decontaminated data, a persistent research record, and a held-out suite the agents are unaware of. In a 104-hour campaign, a team of four Claude Opus 5.5 agents, each on an eight-GPU node and given no research advice, improves Qwen3-4B-Base from 15.9 to 67.3 on a six-task development suite and from 9.2 to 48.2 on a held-out suite, against 66.5 and 47.9 for the official Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that a team of frontier agents can autonomously post-train a raw base model into a cross-domain generalist model that matches or surpasses the official instruct version on average. The campaign's full record shows that the agents reach this level by recombining known methods and each other's models: SFT on public reasoning data, RL with a rollout change one agent introduced, weight merges across agents, and rounds of self-distillation. With execution handled, today's agents already have the engineering and research ability to conquer this task. We hope Athanor provides a foundation for scaling autonomous AI research.

### [StepSafe: Learning Timely Intervention with Context-Aware Safety Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20960v1)

- **Authors:** Siyi Gu, Kunhan Gao, Chao Peter Yang, Sukanta Ganguly, Rex Ying, Arman Cohan
- **Published:** 2026-10-14
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20960v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20960v1)

Safety in agentic systems is inherently sequential: whether an action is safe depends on prior actions, tool outputs, information disclosures, and interactions among agents. Recent work has shifted agent safety guardrails from post-hoc trajectory-level assessment toward step-level action diagnosis and proactive risk prediction, yet existing training objectives provide limited supervision for how step evidence accumulates over time and when it becomes sufficient to justify intervention. We propose \methodlong~(ASPO): a training framework for step-level timely intervention. Pre-fix level stage alignment allows crediting each decision at its own step: it compares each response with the other responses sampled at the same prefix and rewards the action that fits the stage, continuing before the risk is visible, asking while clarification can still avert harm, and stopping before harm is irreversible. Adaptive feedback self-distillation further credits the reasoning behind each decision, with the model itself as a teacher that sees the step annotations on intervention steps and rules mined from its own false alarms on safe steps. Its training data carry a violation taxonomy and an intervention window at every step and are extended through self-evolving multi-agent red-teaming. Finally we present \textsc{StepSafe}, a step-level reasoning guardrail with timely intervention, that achieves the state-of-the-art peformance among open-source guardrails across five agent-safety benchmarks on safety-utility pareto curve with Youden's $J$ of 53.0 (4B) against 46.2.

### [Evolving Together, Failing Apart: The Skill-Policy Co-evolution Trap in Self-Improving Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20845v1)

- **Authors:** Yujie Feng, Xiaobin Wang, Jinluan Yang, Xiaonan Shi, Pengjun Xie, Philip S. Yu, Xiaoming Wu, Jieping Ye
- **Published:** 2026-10-14
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI, cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20845v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20845v1)

Skill-policy co-evolution, in which an agent's policy model and its skill bank improve each other during training, is a promising paradigm for self-improving agents. Current evaluations score the co-evolved policy on the final bank it trained with (the delivered bank). After deployment, however, skills may be rewritten, added or removed more frequently than the policy model is retrained, leaving the policy to operate on a drifted skill bank that neither the training objective nor existing evaluation protocols address. In this paper, we first establish, through theoretical and empirical analysis, that the fragility hidden by this blind spot is not incidental. The acceptance rule these methods share (cooperative acceptance) ties a policy's competence to the bank it co-evolved with, leaving it fragile once skills drift, regardless of the implementation; empirically, sensitivity to skill drift grows as co-evolution training proceeds. We therefore introduce the CO-evolution Drift Audit (CODA), a deployment-oriented evaluation protocol that simulates skill drift and, alongside success rate under worst-case drift, introduces two new metrics: a consistency rate under meaning-preserving edits and a resistance rate to corrupted skills. Across four co-evolution methods and five benchmarks (six settings), worst-case success is 5-13 points below the peak measured for each delivered pair, erasing 52-93% of the delivered bank's gain over the no-skill baseline. CODA thus reframes the target of co-evolution, from peak performance on the co-evolved bank to the ability to harness skills however they evolve, and provides a shared standard against which future methods can be audited.

### [A Self-Improving Agent that Grows its Own DSL: Oracle-Free Operator Induction for Exact Question Answering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20809v1)

- **Authors:** Yun-Shiuan Chuang, Yirou Ge, Yixi Li, Yiming Bian, Zijie Pan, Jenna L. Ballard, Uma Kona, Linsey Pang, Prakhar Mehrotra
- **Published:** 2026-10-14
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI, cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20809v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20809v1)

A self-improving agent has to decide which new skills to keep, and in practice it keeps whatever earns a high reward, convinces a judge model, or compresses its library, even when the skill is wrong. That is fine until the answers have to be exactly right: over a table of transactions, a total is either right or wrong. We ask a harder question: can an agent grow what it can compute with no reward, no judge, and no answer key, and still be exactly right? A single frozen model runs two nested loops over a small, exact domain-specific language (DSL). The inner loop answers new questions only when two independently written expressions agree on every record. The outer loop discovers its own missing capability and authors an operator for it, admitted only when an independent second implementation agrees and the result obeys a conservation law, all without ground truth. The inner loop roughly triples the answerable-question set, and the outer loop self-extends on disjoint starting sets, inventing operators outside the starting closure; a no-induction control answers none of the new questions. The admission rule is empirically sound: it rejects 138 of 138 buggy operator variants under an independent reference, even one written by a different model family, where the admit-if-it-runs, self-consistency, and compression signals the field relies on admit most of them. Unmodified, the same mechanism self-extends on two further domains with unrelated schemas, business-ledger and clinical-encounter question answering, growing their answerable sets more than tenfold and nearly twentyfold. The loop transfers across model families too: run with further proposers, the gate stays sound and independent families self-extend comparably, with only the induction yield tracking each proposer's DSL fluency.

### [EvolvingBrain: An Adaptive Skill System for Multi-modal Agents Self-Evolution in Open Worlds](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20786v1)

- **Authors:** Runxuan Liu, Jingqi Shi, Baoxin Wang, Honghong Zhao, Haoyuan Xu, Yueqing Sun, Jiafeng Liang, Tao He, Dayong Wu, Qi Gu, Shijin Wang, Ming Liu, Bing Qin
- **Published:** 2026-10-14
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20786v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20786v1)

Multi-modal agents have made significant progress in interacting with open worlds. However, their ability to self-evolve remains limited. Supervised fine-tuning enables imitation but not active experience organization, while outcome-based reinforcement learning promotes exploration yet underuses intermediate trajectory information. Therefore, we propose EvolvingBrain, which uses an adaptive skill system as the brain for agent self-evolution, consisting of two following components. Stratified Skill Self-Evolution: extracts Procedural Patterns and Problematic Patterns from model-specific trajectories, summarizes experience by generating skills, and evolves through multiple interactions with the open world, ultimately forming a Stratified Skill Architecture consisting of Domain, Plan, and Action/Trap Skills, which provides the agent with a multi-granularity knowledge repository and a novel evolution approach. State-Aware Adaptive Execution: summarizes long-horizon interactions into Structured Abstractive Memory and uses it to guide the State-Aware Skill Fusion Strategy, enabling the agent to utilize knowledge according to its current state. Experiments on VAB-Minecraft and DiscoveryWorld demonstrate that EvolvingBrain improves success rates on open-world tasks and exhibits generalization capabilities. Our code is provided in the supplementary materials and will be publicly released in the camera-ready version.

### [Co-Evolving Acting and Forecasting for Language Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20597v2)

- **Authors:** Haofei Yu, Yixing Chen, Yining Zhao, Xuyan Ye, Yuxiang Liu, Zhigang Hua, Yan Xie, Shuang Yang, Jiaxuan You
- **Published:** 2026-10-14
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI, cs.LG
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20597v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20597v2)

Training language agents is bottlenecked by the high cost of long-horizon rollouts, yet standard agentic reinforcement learning (RL) learns only from a terminal scalar reward, leaving the supervision in intermediate steps largely unused. Prior work exploits this supervision through value targets that estimate per-step success probabilities or observation targets that fit environment response. Both are hard to construct well: (1) value targets come from the same single scalar signal for each step in the trajectory, and (2) observation targets, such as web pages and terminal logs, are long and noisy and reach only one step ahead. We propose CoPE, which adds a third kind of multi-step action targets. On successful on-policy trajectories, CoPE trains the policy backbone to generate the action sequences that follows each history. Each action is also supervised when forecast from the preceding histories, forming a temporal ensemble that the shared backbone absorbs during training, and this enables acting and forecasting to co-evolve. The policy still acts one step at a time at inference, with no overhead. Across five benchmarks (ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, WebShop, $τ^2$-bench, and AppWorld), CoPE (1) achieves stronger aggregate performance than GRPO and RL baselines with predictive auxiliary targets across simulated environments, while improving overall performance on both realistic applications; (2) delivers up to $1.9\times$ and $2.0\times$ higher sample efficiency than ECHO and GRPO, respectively, on ScienceWorld; and (3) achieves these gains with a single policy backbone, without additional models, separate pretraining, or inference overhead.
