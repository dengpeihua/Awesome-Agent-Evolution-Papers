### [SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41260v2)

- **Authors:** Yiqi Liang, Xiaodong Cun
- **Published:** 2026-10-30
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CV
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41260v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41260v2)

Existing sketch generation methods often rely on image guidance and are largely limited to individual objects or simplified scenes, leaving text-guided complex scene sketching underexplored. We present SketchDirector, an agentic framework that mimics the human sketching workflow for automatic multi-concept scene sketching. Given a scene-level text prompt, SketchDirector first employs a large language model~(LLM) to decompose the scene into multiple draft assets, where each is represented by a set of Bézier curves. Then, an agentic reasoning loop iteratively plans and executes their spatial arrangement, guided by self-evolving memory. Finally, a detail refinement module leverages diffusion priors to enhance sketch quality while preserving geometric structure. To support future research and applications in multi-concept scene sketch generation, we further introduce the MCSketch8K dataset, comprising editable vector assets, compositional layouts, and object-level manipulation annotations. Extensive experiments show that SketchDirector outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in semantic and compositional quality while effectively preserving overall contour organization during refinement.

### [TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnesses to Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41216v2)

- **Authors:** Kehong Gong, Xiaoxu Zheng, Guanli Hou, Seunghun Lee, Mengqi Guo, Runjia Tan, Zhengyu Wen, Qi Wang, Xiaoyu He, Mingyuan Zhang, Michael Bi Mi
- **Published:** 2026-10-30
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.RO, cs.CV
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41216v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41216v2)

Robot agents built on vision-language models depend on both the planner and its harness: the tools, feedback, memory, and recovery logic around it. Can improvements to a large teacher's harness be carried into a compact agent with lower inference compute cost? We present TinyAstral, a two-stage approach. We first freeze an open-weight teacher and improve its harness through recursive self-improvement (RSI): frontier LLM agents read the teacher's trajectories and propose harness edits, and each edit is accepted only if it passes a paired statistical test. We then freeze the final harness, collect teacher trajectories with reasoning and failure recovery, and distill a small student that replaces the teacher in the same harness. On RoboTwin, harness evolution raises the teacher's success from 33.4% to 63.6%; the final 4B student reaches 62.8%. Across four harness versions, distilled 4B students score close to their corresponding teachers, and later versions yield stronger students, showing that harness evolution gains carry into compact models. The harness gains also transfer to other frozen planners without retraining. On identical episodes, the final 4B student outperforms open 4B agents, the open 27B harness-RSI model SenseNova-RoboRSI, and our own teacher model placed in three competitors' harnesses. It stays close to its teacher on all three training benchmarks, and on three benchmarks never used for RSI or training it scores close to a 552B planner running the same harness. On the measured deployment episodes, it uses about one fifth of its teacher's accelerator time. Project page: https://garfield-kh.github.io/TinyAstral/

### [From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41210v1)

- **Authors:** Jun He, Canmiao Fu, Ying Zhang, Zhipeng Huang, Chenyu Mu, Yazhen Xie, Chen Li, Jing Lyu, Weijia Li, Xiang Zhang
- **Published:** 2026-10-30
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CV, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41210v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41210v1)

Real-world image editing requires coordinating multiple visual transformations with content preservation and reference constraints, yet aggregate scores can readily obscure unmet local requirements. Effective evaluation must therefore identify local failures and translate them into priorities for subsequent model improvement. We introduce WeGenEditBench, comprising 2,740 evaluation instances and 148 task types across single-image, text-intensive, and multi-reference editing. Pre-generated checklists shared across models decompose each request into atomic requirements; item-level deductions and visual evidence are established first and then summarized into scores for edit execution, preservation, visual quality, and task-specific correctness. To support the experience-acquisition component of recursive self-improvement (RSI), we develop WeGenEvoStudio, a unified multi-agent harness for RSI-oriented learning that integrates model-conditioned failure diagnosis, retrieval-grounded data construction, supervised adaptation, and re-evaluation. Among 15 evaluated configurations, GPT-Image-2 leads single-image editing with an Overall score of 4.076, while Seedream-5.0-Pro leads text-intensive editing at 4.291. Evolution experiments across four generation and editing benchmarks demonstrate substantial targeted gains: on their report-selected Top-5 single-image targets, FLUX.2 [dev] improves Overall from 1.083 to 3.517, while Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 gains 13.2%. Together, our benchmark and harness provide a foundation for developing more reliable image models through interpretable evaluation and feedback-driven learning.

### [TSFLab: A Self-Improving Agent-Agnostic Workbench for Time Series Forecasting Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40901v1)

- **Authors:** ChengAo Shen, Yanjun Zhao, Haoxin Liu, Bonnie Chen, Miles Ma, Dongjin Song, Hanghang Tong, Qingsong Wen, Jingrui He, Jingchao Ni
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40901v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40901v1)

As publications and products on time series forecasting (TSF) proliferate, researchers face a growing burden of analyzing existing work. First, not all publications release code. For instance, only about 20% of arXiv TSF papers have a code link in the surveyed period. Second, released code also requires manual maintenance. Thus, TSF researchers must constantly adapt or implement existing works. However, independent implementations can differ in experimental settings and are difficult to maintain. We therefore propose to use AI agents for benchmarking TSF research and even supporting autoresearch, e.g., developing new TSF models. Yet directly applying existing coding agents, such as Claude Code and Codex, is suboptimal: prohibitive costs, limited scalability to large research projects (e.g., benchmarking), and lack of self-maintenance. To address these challenges, we propose TSFLab, a self-improving agent-agnostic "workbench" that equips compatible coding agents with shared resources and tools for low-cost TSF research and continuous maintenance of research resources. Our experiments cover reproduction, benchmarking, and new method development. In reproduction and benchmarking, TSFLab improves task completion by 20-163% and cuts fresh tokens per completed task by 11-66% across baselines. A pilot study of agent-driven method development also shows improved forecasting accuracy via TSFLab, with 32.8-73.6% fewer fresh tokens and 9.2-68.8% less development time than the other settings.

### [More Rules, Longer Lists? Benchmarking Self-Evolving Agents on a Hidden-Rule Curriculum](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40758v1)

- **Authors:** Chenjie Qiu, Boyan Xu, Shaobin Shi, Bingfeng Chen, Ruichu Cai, Dongsheng Luo
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40758v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40758v1)

Self-evolving agents rewrite a persistent state from feedback, but it is largely untested whether this lets them learn a rule nobody told them. An agent can also pass by writing down each correction as an exception, and its list then grows with every new rule. We introduce CLR-Bench, a Curriculum of Latent Rules benchmark. Each chain adds one hidden rule per level on top of the earlier ones. A rule acts on an opaque label that stands for a named grouping published on the Web, such as the founding members of an airline alliance. The task gives one example value, practice feedback reveals one more member and one non-member, and the test checks unseen members and similar non-members. Probes certify that the base model knows the members of each grouping and does not guess it without feedback, and that the evidence identifies the grouping once labels are read as named groupings. An automated pipeline builds chains from a new data domain, and we release 20 chains over 10 domains. We evaluate six recent self-evolution methods. With the certifying model they barely learn. The best gain at most 5 points over a frozen agent and exclude only 6-12% of the unseen members from the count, fewer than the 20% reached by simply keeping the raw practice history. A fixed lesson that states the named-grouping convention lifts the same methods by 14.5 to 16.5 points. Their states hold the revealed member in 75-98% of cases and almost never the class name, and when practice exposes a member the list misses, they add that member to the list. With the stronger GPT-6-Sol, ACE and Dynamic Cheatsheet learn from feedback alone. They reach 78% accuracy and solve 59-60% of the single cells, against 53% and 8% with the certifying model, and they surpass the raw history. CLR-Bench therefore measures how well an agent learns, and this ability differs across models.

### [Safin-1.5: Safety-Native Architecture and Verifiable Reasoning for Recursive Safety Improvement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40751v1)

- **Authors:** Jingnan Zheng, Cheng Jin, Ming Zhang, Huan Sun, Zhongtian Ma, Kaisen Yang, Ziyuan Zhou, Tong Wu, Dongcheng Zhang, Yi Zhang, Xiao Wang, Han Qi, Shu Yu, Ermo Hua, Zongji Yu, Chaozhuo Li, Xiaoyu Wen, Zhekai Chen, Jiawei Zhou, Sirui Chen, Ning Ding, An Zhang, Tat-Seng Chua, Xiangnan He, Xia Hu, Bowen Zhou, Qiaosheng Zhang, Youbang Sun, Xiang Wang, Zhongyu Huang, Chaochao Lu
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CR, cs.AI, cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40751v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40751v1)

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) enables large language models to advance their capabilities through successive updates, but these updates can also introduce new risks and weaken existing safeguards. In line with the AI-45° Law, we pursue Safety RSI, in which safety develops as an intrinsic capability alongside general model capabilities through continual risk discovery and repair, while preserving utility and limiting over-refusal. We introduce Safin-1.5, a model built on a safety architecture and trained through recursive safety improvement, with an inference-time harness that extends its capabilities to formal reasoning. Our approach comprises three components: i) The safety architecture combines persistent safety states with hazardous-knowledge disentanglement. The former retains and selectively invokes safety knowledge, while the latter relocates targeted hazardous knowledge into removable experts, enabling its removal with minimal impact on general capabilities. ii) Recursive safety improvement establishes a discovery-evaluation-repair loop. The loop identifies new vulnerabilities and uses experimental feedback to develop repair strategies. It accepts updates only when they improve safety while preserving general capabilities and limiting over-refusal. iii) The formal reasoning harness combines the model's language understanding with symbolic inference and verification. Proof-obligation-guided semantic bridging recovers missing relations in formal representations, enabling symbolic solvers to construct checkable proofs under formalized premises and accepted semantic bridges. Experimental results demonstrate that Safin-1.5 achieves safety and reasoning performance, outperforming frontier models.

### [Video Generation Meets Agent Harness: Agentic Video Generation, Agentic World Modeling and Recursive Self-Improvement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40191v1)

- **Authors:** Liaoyuan Fan, Yuhao Zheng, Dongxing Mao, Hangyu Ran, Siyuan Hu, Weijia Mao, Rui Zhao, Weijia Wu, Guian Fang, Yanzhe Chen, Zeyu Zhu, Yuxin Jiang, Yiren Song, Yuchao Gu, Junchao Yi, Qi Hu, Dan Kong, Alex Jinpeng Wang, Kevin Qinghong Lin, Mike Zheng Shou, Wei Liu
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CV
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40191v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40191v1)

Video generation is shifting from end-to-end synthesis toward agentic generation, where planning, tool use, and feedback enable coherent, controllable multi-shot videos. In parallel, video world models provide action-conditioned observations and predicted futures, serving as interactive environments for agents. We study their intersection through the agent harness: an executable runtime that constructs context, exposes tools, maintains state, routes feedback, and manages budgets around video models. This report examines three directions. First, agentic video generation, where harnesses coordinate scripting, shot planning, asset management, production, and editing, alongside the evaluation challenges of these systems. Second, agentic world modeling, where harnesses mediate observation, control, execution, and state. We discuss hybrid designs in which game engines or agent-written programs maintain world state while video models render observations, and how agents and world models can mutually evaluate and improve one another. Third, recursive self-improvement (RSI) in video model development, distinguishing output revisions, updates to harness state, generator parameters, and evaluators, and changes to the improvement procedure itself. We identify how these improvements feed back into subsequent development. Across these directions, we analyze essential agent capabilities, including audiovisual perception, long-horizon planning, memory, code-based interaction, feedback-guided control, skill reuse, and multi-agent coordination. We conclude with challenges in autonomous video production, lifelong memory, programmable hybrid control, autonomous model development, provenance, cost accounting, and evaluation linking video quality and task performance to sustained improvements in models and harnesses.

### [HEMO: Harness Self-Evolution for Multi-Objective Combinatorial Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40023v1)

- **Authors:** Jianghan Zhu, Cong Zhang, Ni Zhang, Yifan Yang, Yew Soon Ong, Zhiguang Cao
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.NE, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40023v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40023v1)

Automatic operator design (AOD) has increasingly adopted large language models (LLMs) to generate problem-specific search and variation operators for multi-objective combinatorial optimization (MOCO). Yet most methods evolve the operators while leaving the process that discovers them fixed. This limits adaptation as new Pareto trade-offs and operator interactions change where and how the search should proceed. We introduce HEMO, which makes the AOD harness itself a self-evolving optimization target. HEMO maintains a persistent harness state and updates one module at a time, revising the logic that determines which experience to retrieve, which operators to improve, which parents to select, and how to formulate the orchestration of search steps. Downstream solution quality and runtime guide these updates, while a discounted UCB scheduler directs adaptation toward promising modules as their utility changes. A persistent memory filesystem preserves candidate outcomes and diagnostic feedback across harness states, enabling successive states to build on accumulated optimization experience. Experiments across eight MOCO tasks and two structurally distinct evolutionary backbones demonstrate strong performance against conventional evolutionary, neural, and LLM-based AOD baselines, together with effective transfer to related tasks. HEMO extends automatic operator design beyond evolving individual operators to improving the search process that discovers them.

### [KARMA: Training-Free Knowledge-Atomic Harness Evolution for Industrial Advertising Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39884v2)

- **Authors:** Xinhua Miao, Yuanzhen Rong, Weiru Zhang, Xiaoyi Zeng
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39884v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39884v2)

Industrial advertising agents must continually adapt their decision knowledge to changing product offerings, traffic patterns, and operating policies. We present KARMA (Knowledge-Atomic, Regression-Gated Modular Adaptation), a training-free framework that evolves six typed knowledge modules while keeping model weights and executable harness code frozen. During our evolution, a deterministic Trace Verifier maps observed errors to candidate modules, and a Critic compares successful and failed trajectories to guide a tool-constrained Evolver. The Evolver applies atomic knowledge edits, which a fixed validation gate retains only when their measured gain reaches a threshold; otherwise, the previous knowledge state is restored. We evaluate KARMA with three LLM families on cases sampled from a corpus of approximately half a million real-world operation logs across six e-commerce domains. Across 18 domain--model settings, KARMA raises the average hold-out score from 30.19 to 39.15 (+8.96 points, +29.7%). Ablations show that removing either successful or failed trajectories more than halves the average hold-out gain. Evolved knowledge also transfers across domains without further adaptation, yielding an average hold-out gain of 8.54 points across all source--target pairs. By linking retained patches to their diagnostic evidence and validation outcomes, KARMA supports inspectable, reversible knowledge adaptation for agents in Web marketplaces.

- **Authors:** Alexander Detkov, Dashiel Karish, Matthew Oleynikov, Matt Thomson
- **Published:** 2026-10-30
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41169v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41169v1)

The ability to integrate new information and deduce its consequences is central to learning, understanding, and reasoning in large language models (LLMs). Whether information arrives during training or in-context, models must not only store local facts, but propagate their long-range implications globally. To explore information integration in LLMs, we introduce two complementary natural language benchmarks for large-scale evaluation of deductive reasoning as a function of inference depth. The first is a formal graph benchmark enabling controlled evaluation over a wide range of graph topologies and integration tasks; the second is a knowledge composition benchmark grounded in real-world knowledge graphs. We use these benchmarks to compare knowledge integration from information encoded in context versus information encoded in model weights during pretraining, evaluating a set of open models implicitly, without chain of thought. We find that accuracy decays sharply with increased composition length for both pretrained and in-context knowledge. Information presented in-context, however, substantially outperforms deduction over information encoded in weights. These results quantify a fundamental limitation of current models with direct implications for prompt-based control of agent behavior and for continual learning strategies, and they provide a foundation for developing training, prompting, and integration methods that support deeper reasoning.

### [Frozen in Time: How a Physics Foundation Model Forgets Under Sim-to-Real Adaptation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40857v1)

- **Authors:** Haaris Mian, Miles Cranmer, Alessandro Favero, Payel Mukhopadhyay
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, physics.flu-dyn
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40857v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40857v1)

Scientific foundation models (SciFMs) have achieved remarkable performance on a wide range of PDEs, including the ability to efficiently adapt to unseen data distributions with additional finetuning. As with all neural architectures, finetuning such models comes with the risk of catastrophic forgetting, whereby adapting to new tasks degrades prior knowledge of the model. We study catastrophic forgetting in Walrus-1.3B, a SciFM for continuum dynamics, over the course of a two-stage sim-to-real adaptation: first finetuning on simulated flow around a cylinder and then adapting to experimental data of the same system. We find that finetuning on the simulations rapidly degrades rollout performance on the original pretraining datasets, with one-step errors increasing up to 35x after just 20 epochs. Subsequent adaptation to real data further erodes performance on the simulated task. We characterize the potential drivers of forgetting, such as dataset dependence and task representation similarity. We then show that replaying as little as 1% simulated data during real-data finetuning recovers most of the lost simulation performance while retaining effective adaptation to the experimental dynamics. Our results identify catastrophic forgetting as a practical obstacle to sim-to-real adaptation of SciFMs and show that lightweight replay strategies significantly improve retention.

### [Training Foundation Models with a Single Algorithm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40564v1)

- **Authors:** Yuda Song, Alvin Zhang, Fahim Tajwar, Daman Arora, Jeff Schneider, Daniel Khashabi, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, J. Andrew Bagnell, Aarti Singh, Andrea Zanette
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40564v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40564v1)

Foundation models are typically trained in stages: pretraining, midtraining, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL), with changes in both data and learning algorithms. We show that this algorithmic separation is not fundamental. By deriving suitable RL objectives as maximum-likelihood fitting to model-generated target distributions, we place all of these stages under a single likelihood-based training algorithm, unified training. This perspective turns the foundation-model training lifecycle into a problem of choosing which data to train on, in what proportions, and when, with conventional staged training as a special case. We instantiate this framework in a unified training system that mixes static data and RL generations within a single trainer. Across controlled and real-world experiments, unified training improves the accuracy-compute tradeoff and task coverage while preserving broader capabilities, and provides a stronger starting point for continual learning. This work lays the groundwork for a unified science of foundation-model training, connecting mathematical principles, training infrastructure, and the development of model capabilities.

### [Certified Behavioral Retention for Continual Adaptation with Held-Out Anchors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40740v1)

- **Authors:** Ahanaf Hasan Ariq, Abrar Shahid
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, stat.ML
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40740v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40740v1)

Continual adaptation requires deciding not only how to update a model, but whether to commit an update without exceeding a declared budget for changes to protected behavior. We introduce Certified Anchor-Based Adaptation (CABA), a candidate-agnostic post-update gate that reserves historical observations from candidate optimization, compares old and candidate policies on frozen representations, and selects the largest tested interpolation coefficient whose worst-anchor forward KL divergence remains within budget. The finite check requires neither action labels nor complete trajectories. We develop a theory-led certification framework that separates measured anchor retention from conditional off-anchor guarantees. Under explicit representation-coverage and Lipschitz assumptions, an accepted update satisfies a uniform behavioral-divergence bound on protected support. The analysis gives regularity bounds for affine softmax heads, probabilistic coverage and compression guarantees, interpolation monotonicity conditions, grid-resolution bounds, and trajectory-level and sequential-composition guarantees. In a controlled UCI Human Activity Recognition study used as a non-stationary sensor-policy proxy, CABA reduces mean forgetting from 1.01 percent for unconstrained adaptation to 0.00 percent at budget epsilon = 0.05, while reducing final average accuracy from 93.04 percent to 88.48 percent. The experiment illustrates a retention-plasticity trade-off and does not establish foundation-model or robotic performance. CABA provides an auditable retention check for candidate generators exposing comparable policy distributions and a declared update path, while deployment also requires separate evidence of current-task utility.

### [KARMA: Training-Free Knowledge-Atomic Harness Evolution for Industrial Advertising Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39884v2)

- **Authors:** Xinhua Miao, Yuanzhen Rong, Weiru Zhang, Xiaoyi Zeng

Industrial advertising agents must continually adapt their decision knowledge to changing product offerings, traffic patterns, and operating policies. We present KARMA (Knowledge-Atomic, Regression-Gated Modular Adaptation), a training-free framework that evolves six typed knowledge modules while keeping model weights and executable harness code frozen. During our evolution, a deterministic Trace Verifier maps observed errors to candidate modules, and a Critic compares successful and failed trajectories to guide a tool-constrained Evolver. By linking retained patches to their diagnostic evidence and validation outcomes, KARMA supports inspectable, reversible knowledge adaptation for agents in Web marketplaces.

### [Surprise Alone Learns Lies: Evidence-Gated Continual Learning for LLMs from Streaming Claims](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39840v1)

- **Authors:** Abdelkader Metales, Miriam Grabenheinrich
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.CL, cs.CR
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39840v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39840v1)

Continual learning methods for large language models increasingly decide what to learn by surprise, keeping the inputs the model finds least predictable. This rule is unsafe when the stream contains lies. A planted falsehood about something the model knows has low probability under the model's belief, so it is highly surprising by construction, and a surprise-driven learner absorbs nearly every such lie, at a rate similar to random selection with the same budget. We study the problem with LoRA-adapted students (Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-3B and Llama-3.2-3B) that learn from a stream of true and false claims about invented people and real CounterFact facts. We report four findings. (1) A verifier key, a judge that must approve each claim, removes lies to the extent that its verifier is accurate; real LLM judges are cautious and reject unsourced truths and even source-backed updates. (2) Calibrated regression tests on unrelated knowledge slow learning down without filtering out lies. (3) The original model's own prior flags claims that contradict what it knows, without a judge. It separates lies about known facts from true new facts (AUC 0.78-0.83 across three models) but not from legitimate updates of the same kind of fact (AUC 0.53). It saves 43% of judge calls, but an attacker with access to the model can choose lies that pass it. (4) If trusted sources exist that an attacker cannot plant, routing contradicted claims to evidence recovers legitimate updates and the combined system has the lowest poison rate among learned claims; when an attacker plants matching sources for 30% of the lies, the system ends up worse than the judge alone. The acceptance signals answer different questions, and none of them decides on its own whether a claim is true.

### [bf16 Rounding Silently Discards Most RL Policy Updates: A Hidden Training-Inference Mismatch and an Unbiased One-Line Fix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41073v1)

- **Authors:** Andrew Gao, William Gao
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41073v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.41073v1)

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training of language models keeps fp32 master weights in the optimizer and serves rollouts from a bf16 copy produced by round-to-nearest conversion. At the learning rates in common use, most per-step updates are smaller than half a bf16 spacing and the conversion discards them: on Qwen3-1.7B only 0.4-0.5% of served weights change per step, and the policy that generates the training data lags the optimizer by 50-150 steps. Existing mismatch corrections cannot see this lag because they compare two copies downstream of the same rounding. We propose Fair Cast: export the master with an unbiased rounding rule (stochastic rounding or error feedback) and compute the training loss on the served copy. The change is one line at the export call, adds no hyperparameters and costs 22-38 ms per export of 1.7B parameters. Across 25 matched seed-pairs on Qwen3-0.6B to 4B with AdamW and Muon, Fair Cast is ahead on 18, tied on 6 and behind on 1, with a mean gain of 6.1 points; on zebra puzzles it reaches 0.57 +/- 0.16 over four runs where round-to-nearest collapses to 0.33 +/- 0.10. It also makes the saved fp32 checkpoint agree with the served model, and it exposes the implicit lag of round-to-nearest as a regularizer that can be restored explicitly when a task needs it.

### [Steering Vectors as a Training Signal in LLM Post-Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40998v1)

- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40998v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40998v1)

Steering vectors (SVs) are residual-stream directions that shift the behavior of LLMs when added to hidden states at inference time. While prior work has mainly used SVs as post-hoc controls, we ask whether the same directions can also guide parameter updates during post-training. ### [On the Origin and Evolution of Training-Inference Mismatch in LLM Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40945v1)

- **Authors:** Yu Li, Yufa Zhou, Tian Lan
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40945v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40945v1)

On-policy reinforcement learning for large language models assumes that training responses are sampled from the current policy. In practice, an inference engine samples the responses and a separate trainer computes the gradients, and the \textbf{training-inference mismatch} between the two makes the training data off-policy. The mismatch has two sources, the numerics of the engine and the stale weights of asynchronous sampling, and a correction that stabilizes training under one source can fail under the other. \textit{We ask which property of a mismatch source decides the correction it needs.} Evaluating the trainer at the sampler's weights splits each sampled-token error exactly into a \textit{numerical discrepancy} of the engine and a \textit{policy lag} of the trainer, and we follow both through training. The numerical discrepancy stays approximately constant, so it biases the updates by a nearly fixed amount, and centering the score at each prefix removes its leading bias. The policy lag is produced by the trainer's own updates: the bias it leaves enters the next update, which enlarges the next lag and closes a feedback loop. Importance weighting cuts this loop only while the lag ratio stays concentrated, and on Qwen3-1.7B it fails at the step where the effective sample size of the ratio falls below one half. From this account we derive the \textbf{discrepancy-lag correction} (DLC), which centers the numerical discrepancy, clips the lag ratio against the sampler's weights and resynchronizes the sampler when this effective sample size falls below one half. Under a stale sampler, DLC keeps the KL divergence from on-policy training within twice the KL between two independent on-policy trainings on Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B, and its resynchronization prevents collapses that the per-token corrections leave and that a KL measured on the sampler's prefixes does not detect.

### [There Is No Free Lunch in RLVR: Every pass@1 Gain Has a pass@k Price](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39295v1)

- **Authors:** Yinuo Liu
- **Published:** 2026-10-29
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI, math.OC, stat.ML
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39295v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.39295v1)

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) makes a language model solve more problems on the first try and fewer when given many tries: it raises pass@1 while the base model overtakes it at large sampling budget k. Is this a failure of RL to add reasoning, or a transient that more training removes? We settle the question with a theorem. The reversal is a price, and we compute its exact rate. Modeling RLVR as an exponential tilt of a base policy over strategies shared across prompts, we prove that under exclusive specialization every update that raises pass@1 strictly lowers the success of some prompt; a strictly positive price on prompts (Stiemke duality) is the exact condition, and being merely the best strategy on a prompt is provably not enough. When the loss reaches the hardest prompts, it forces a pass@k crossover, and the number of crossings is at most the sign variation of the shift in per-prompt success, a totally positive bound that is tight and that recovers the known single-crossover result as its order-preserving case. The crossover is not a bug to patch: a constrained update traces the exact pass@1 versus pass@k frontier, and removing the crossover entirely costs the entire pass@1 gain. The same tilt identity yields entropy collapse and the entropy-performance law with an identifiable exponent, unifying two literatures under one mechanism. Reinforcement cannot widen the reasoning frontier; it can only move along it, at a price we make measurable by a diagnostic that reads the bound, the order-preserving test, and the price from any evaluation log. There is no free lunch, only a price.

### [Reasoning or Pattern Exploitation? Mechanistic Insights from RL-Trained Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39182v1)

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly described as acquiring "reasoning" skills after reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Long-horizon LLM agents play an important role in solving complex and sustained tasks, with ever-growing interaction contexts. However, these accumulated contexts will raise substantial computational cost and obscure key decision-relevant evidence.
