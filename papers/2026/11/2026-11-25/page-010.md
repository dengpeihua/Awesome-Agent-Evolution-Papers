### [Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates Lose Redundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38392v1)

- **Authors:** Hau-Sing So, Daixian Liu, Weicai Long
- **Published:** 2026-10-28
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38392v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.38392v1)

Self-improving LLM agents edit their own harness, the prompts and rules around a fixed model, and keep an edit only if an acceptance gate scores it higher on a batch of tasks. Such a gate sees what the harness does under the current model, not what it would still prevent if one safeguard failed. When safeguards overlap, deleting one is masked: the others still block the harm, so the gate decides on task success alone and redundancy erodes unseen. We measure harm for every subset of four written safeguards in two agent environments and run 411 improvement loops under ten gate rules, mostly as controlled deletion experiments, plus three runs of the GEPA optimizer. A deletion behind another working safeguard adds almost no observable harm. A paired significance test blocks deletions that pass by chance but not deletions that genuinely raise task success, and a larger evaluation batch lets it delete cheaper safeguards: at the same cost, 20-task batches kept every harness protected while 40-task batches lost all six. Large harm audits prevent exposure but not the loss of depth, which turns into harm when the harness is reused with a weaker model. GEPA kept every safety rule verbatim, yet two of its three final harnesses misused credentials under a weaker model. A statistical model frozen before the runs predicts where each gate's protection breaks as safeguard cost and batch size change, and freezing safety text or auditing one-layer ablations preserves depth.

### [FilingLoop: Evaluating and Evolving LLM Agents for Corporate Disclosure Review and Revision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37905v1)

- **Authors:** Wentao Zhang, Yilei Zhao, Guangyan Gan, Shuo Sun, Lin William Cong
- **Published:** 2026-10-27
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37905v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.37905v1)

Public disclosure repositories such as SEC EDGAR support informed investment through web access to corporate filings. Supporting their review with language model agents requires discovering unstated deficiencies across related documents, acquiring missing company facts, and verifying that requested revisions satisfy evidence-backed requirements. Existing financial question-answering evaluations provide limited evidence of these interactive capabilities. We introduce \textbf{FilingLoop}, an interactive benchmark and a self-evolving agent framework for corporate disclosure review and revision. Grounded in historical U.S.\ Securities and Exchange Commission correspondence, FilingLoop reconstructs 380 predominantly IPO review tasks from 96 companies under distinct information constraints. An evaluator separate from the acting agents scores final filings and responses against 6,099 eligible requirements and audits unsupported claims and harmful revisions. Our framework couples iterative review and revision within each case with procedural learning across cases, converting requirement-level evaluation feedback into role-specific guidance without updating model weights. Updates affect only subsequent cases, allowing procedural adaptation to be assessed without retrying scored tasks. On the evaluated stream, evolution increases requirement fulfillment from 24.28\% to 27.96\% and full comment resolution from 16.90\% to 20.75\%, with a 5.8\% increase in total cost over the fixed-policy variant. FilingLoop connects the concerns an agent discovers with the company facts it obtains and the revisions it commits, providing requirement-level feedback for procedures used in later review tasks.

### [GEPA-Curator: Curated Learning for Self Improving Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37651v1)

- **Authors:** Zack Yu, Nicholas Lee, Minjae Lee, Lakshya Agarwal, Luca Manolache, Wonjun Kang, Matei Zaharia, Michael W. Mahoney, Kurt Keutzer, Sehoon Kim, Amir Gholami
- **Published:** 2026-10-27
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37651v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.37651v1)

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in multi-turn agentic workloads that require sustained interaction with complex environments. Existing reflective prompt optimizers such as GEPA learn from failed rollouts, but agentic failures can also come from prompt weaknesses, harness limitations, stochastic execution, or noisy judges. We argue that such failures may not provide useful learning signal, and may even be harmful; and thus it is essential to curate which tasks are provided to the prompt optimizer for better performance. We introduce GEPA-Curator, a framework that augments the prompt optimizer to select which agent experiences should shape prompt optimization. In particular, GEPA-Curator consists of two components: task curation, which filters out tasks that are likely to provide noisy or unreliable learning signals, and prior success retrieval, which selectively retrieves past successful trajectories for a task and provides them as additional context to the prompt optimizer. We evaluate GEPA-Curator on GoBrowse, ALFWorld, AppWorld, and $τ$-bench, using both Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 5 as optimizer models. GEPA-Curator matches or exceeds the mean performance of both baselines, with gains of up to 20.7% over GEPA and 21.2% over ACE.

### [AutoRSE: Evaluating LLM Agents as Recommendation Engineers for Self-Evolving Recommender Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37228v2)

- **Authors:** Rui Tian, Yi Fang, Yejin Hua, Du Wang, Yuzhe Wang, Minxuan Shan, Zirui Ren, Xinyu Lin, Chongming Gao, Wenjie Wang, Fuli Feng
- **Published:** 2026-10-27
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.IR
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37228v2) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.37228v2)

Recommender Systems Engineer Agents (RSE-Agents), which use LLM-based agents to autonomously improve recommendation algorithms, are emerging as a promising paradigm. However, their capabilities and limitations remain poorly understood due to the lack of a unified, standardized evaluation framework and systematic trajectory-level analysis. To address this gap, we introduce AutoRSE, an evaluation framework spanning six representative industrial-grade recommendation tasks, and conduct a large-scale empirical study of three frontier LLMs across three widely used agent harnesses. Our results show that RSE-Agents consistently enhance the performance of backbones on five of the six tasks, with an average relative improvement of 16.1%, indicating that they can already autonomously improve recommendation performance. However, trajectory-level analysis also reveals that current RSE-Agents function primarily as engineering optimizers rather than algorithmic improvers: their gains are largely driven by hyperparameter tuning, training strategies, and model ensembling, while genuine algorithmic innovations are rare. Since the largest gains in recommender systems often stem from algorithmic advances, this behavior underscores a fundamental limitation of current RSE-Agents. To probe this limitation, we perform idea-injection experiments and show that agents equipped with innovative ideas from frontier research or human experts achieve substantially larger improvements than those relying on self-generated ideas. These results suggest that idea generation is a central bottleneck of current RSE-Agents, highlights a key direction for the future recommendation system research. Our implementation is publicly available on GitHub: https://github.com/Ray-Tiann/AutoRSE.

### [Evolving Agent Teams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36848v1)

- **Authors:** Shiyi Cao, Ziming Mao, Dacheng Li, Joseph E. Gonzalez, Ion Stoica
- **Published:** 2026-10-27
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36848v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36848v1)

The rapid progress of software engineering agents drives interest in applying them to open-ended problems such as GPU kernel optimization. However, we find that existing coding agents converge to local optima on real-world, expert-written kernels (e.g., FlashInfer). We introduce Evolving Agent Teams, a multi-agent framework that escapes such local optima. It features (1) a central supervisor that diagnoses each local optimum and restructures a team of agents; and (2) a persistent file system that comprises a learning document, an evolution history, and per-team artifacts, grounding restructuring with accumulated experience. Under a matched token budget, Evolving Agent Teams consistently outperforms strong coding-agent baselines. With an extended token budget, Evolving Agent Teams generates a CUDA kernel that reaches 1.2x mean speedup over the production FlashInfer MLA paged decode kernel from scratch and wins on 36 of 47 production workloads from FlashInfer-Bench (0.86-0.99x on the remaining 11). It also demonstrates superior performance on tasks such as KDA prefill on AMD MI350X (3.01x over vLLM) and NVIDIA B200 (3.44x over FlashKDA), MoE expert-offload serving on Apple silicon (30.3 tokens/s decode for Qwen3.8-Flash-Next in MLX 4-bit on a 48 GB M4 Pro), and RoboDojo robot manipulation (62.1 on held-out seeds vs. 55.3 for the best leaderboard entry). Code is available at https://github.com/caoshiyi/EAT.

### [When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36826v1)

- **Authors:** Hao Chen, Yukun Yan, Ziyu Han, Ye He, Chuanzhe Guo, Shangzhan Li, Qi Shi, Qingfu Zhu, Maosong Sun, Wanxiang Che
- **Published:** 2026-10-27
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36826v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36826v1)

Autonomous agents increasingly depend on harnesses that govern interaction, memory, tools, and execution around large language models (LLMs). While recent work has begun to evolve these harnesses from execution experience, the supervision used to guide evolution often remains relatively static, making it difficult to track changing behavioral weaknesses across rounds and potentially limiting generalization to unseen tasks. To address this issue, we propose RuHarness, a rubric--harness co-evolution framework for continual agent self-improvement. RuHarness continually discovers and reassesses behavioral criteria from execution trajectories, prioritizes the most relevant unresolved weaknesses, and uses them to guide targeted harness updates while keeping the task objective fixed. Extensive experiments across diverse agent tasks and backbone models show that RuHarness outperforms strong harness-evolution baselines, particularly on unseen held-out tasks. Further analyses show that adaptive rubric prioritization tracks shifting weaknesses and supports more sustained improvement and stable generalization across evolution rounds.

### [The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36660v3)

- **Authors:** Shuo Yan, Ningning Hou, Haoyu He, Haochen Pei, Shang Gao
- **Published:** 2026-10-27
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.AI
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36660v3) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36660v3)

Self-improving AI systems can revise the designs that govern their own behavior. Yet existing approaches center improvement on selected parent agents and their evolutionary experience, leaving the broader population largely underused. We identify a substantial gap between population coverage (i.e., the tasks solved by at least one evolved variant) and the performance of the best individual agent. This gap shows that useful capabilities and diagnostic evidence emerge across the population without being consolidated into its leading agents. These observations motivate the view from nowhere, a principle for agent evolution: an agent should be improved by interpreting its behavior through population-wide patterns of success, failure, and prior self-modification. We instantiate this principle as Population-Centered Evolution (PCE). PCE distils task records and modification histories from the population into a population block, which captures recurring failures, complementary successes, and the effects of previously attempted modifications to guide improvements to a selected parent's harness. Under matched models, initial harnesses, and evolution schedules, PCE achieves 72.1% success on SWE-bench Verified and 96.7% on Polyglot, improving over a strong group-evolution baseline by 3.2 and 5.9 percentage points. Across independent runs, PCE reduces the gap between population coverage and best-agent performance by 30% on SWE-bench and all but closes it on Polyglot. These results suggest that treating the population as evidence, not merely as candidates for selection, provides a complementary principle for agent evolution.

### [AudioRSI: Recursively Self-Improving Audio Reasoners](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36578v1)

- **Authors:** Jiajun Fan, Roger Ren, Jingyuan Li, Rahul Pandey, Prashanth Gurunath Shivakumar, Ankur Gandhe, Yile Gu, Ge Liu, Ivan Bulyko
- **Published:** 2026-10-27
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.SD
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36578v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36578v1)

Reinforcement learning has made Audio Large Language Models (Audio LLMs) better reasoners, but the reward that drives it still comes from outside the model: a final-answer check, a hand-crafted process score, or an external LLM judge that stays fixed while the policy improves. We show that an Audio LLM can supply this reward itself, and can do so recursively: each improved model becomes the critic that trains its successor, so the training signal improves along with the model. We introduce AudioRSI (Recursively Self-Improving Audio Reasoners), a reinforcement learning framework in which a single model plays two roles: as an actor it generates reasoning paths, and as a critic it compares them pairwise, turning its own judgments into a process-level reward. The critic is periodically replaced by the current policy, which closes the recursive loop. Because the critic hears the same recording as the actor, it can reject reasoning that cites acoustic evidence the audio does not contain, rather than reward reasoning that merely sounds plausible. The recursion is what sustains progress: under a frozen external judge, out-of-distribution accuracy plateaus during training, while a critic refreshed from the improving policy keeps it rising. Across four out-of-distribution benchmarks, all disjoint from the AVQA training distribution, AudioRSI outperforms strong baselines including Audio Flamingo 3 and Gemini 2.5 Pro on MMAU test-mini, ranks first on MMSU, achieves a clear lead on the challenging Multi-Audio split of MMAU-Pro, and leads the 7B open-source tier on MMAR among methods without external reward models. With no reward specifying how to reason, AudioRSI also develops structured reasoning behaviors (logical reasoning, perception-aware analysis, and reduced hallucination), corroborated by an external LLM-as-Judge evaluation and a keyword-based behavior analysis.

### [CoHARL: Co-Evolving Agent Policies and Executable Harnesses with Reinforcement Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36469v1)

- **Authors:** Jianwen Chen, Peng Xia, Kaide Zeng, Siwei Han, Zhen-Yu Zhang, Charles Fleming, Xinyu Ye, Huaxiu Yao
- **Published:** 2026-10-27
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36469v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36469v1)

Large language model (LLM) agents interact with their environments through executable harnesses that govern tool use and feedback. Optimizing a harness for a fixed policy overlooks how its behavior changes during learning, while training a policy under a fixed harness leaves the interface that generates its training experience unchanged. This motivates adapting the harness throughout policy training. We introduce CoHARL, a population-based reinforcement learning framework that co-evolves a shared policy and a population of executable harnesses. The policy learns from rollouts across the population, and a proposer uses its execution traces to revise harness code during training. CoHARL achieves a 27.6% mean resolved rate on SWE-rebench, improving over harness-only search by 6.4 percentage points. Experiments on SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2.0 show gains in software engineering and terminal-based tasks. Under matched training budgets, CoHARL also outperforms searching for a harness once before RL and alternating policy updates with revisions to a single active harness.

### [Training for Alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40684v1)

- **Authors:** Eric Gao, Alexander Wolitzky
- **Published:** 2026-10-26
- **Priority:** —
- **Venue:** arXiv
- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** econ.TH
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40684v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.40684v1)

We propose a simple model of training an agent with an endogenous bias. A principal chooses in which states to train the agent and what actions to prescribe in these states. In response, the agent updates their bias and then acts optimally given their new bias in untrained states, while following their training in trained states. The agent chooses their new bias to minimize a weighted average of their loss under their initial and final biases. Training can thus generalize to untrained states via the agent's bias updating. We characterize optimal training, which involves \emph{over-correction} in trained states and \emph{generalization-aware} (non-greedy) selection of training states. If only a small share of states can be trained, greedy state selection is approximately optimal, but over-correction remains essential. If any bias function is possible, or if the principal evaluates training robustly over bias functions that admit the possibility of full alignment, the value of training generalization vanishes, and greedy training becomes optimal. With a more pessimistic objective, \emph{under-correction}, or even no training at all, may be optimal. The principal benefits from the agent's preferences being more flexible (\emph{corrigibility}) when the set of possible biases is known and contains full alignment, but otherwise corrigibility can be harmful. Applications include socialization in organizations and families, and AI post-training and recursive self-improvement.

### [Select-TTT: Query-Conditioned Selective Fast Weight Updates for In-Place Test-Time Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36794v1)

- **Affiliations:** n/a
- **Categories:** cs.LG, cs.AI, cs.CL
- **Links:** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36794v1) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36794v1)

Test-time training (TTT) enables a deployed language model to internalize incoming context on-the-fly into a small set of fast-weight parameters, rather than merely treating it as transient context for attention. In-Place TTT recently made this idea practical for large language models by repurposing the down-projection of standard MLP blocks as fast weights. Select-TTT consistently improves long-context question answering on two benchmarks and two backbones. These findings highlight the value of query-conditioned fast weight writes for practical test-time training on long-context question answering, which is also potentially promising for long-horizon agent tasks involving multiple user queries over evolving contexts.
